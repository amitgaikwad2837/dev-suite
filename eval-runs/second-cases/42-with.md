The survey is the wrong first step for "why". I'd swap it for interviews and use a short survey later only to size what you find.

**Why the survey won't answer your question**

- **It measures how common, not why.** A multiple-choice survey can only report reasons you already thought of. If the real reason is something you didn't list ("my fridge was full of unused kits", "my partner stopped eating them"), 20 questions won't surface it. People also pick a tidy answer such as "too expensive" because it's easy to give, even when it isn't the true cause.
- **Who answers is skewed.** Lapsed users who open a marketing email and take a survey are mostly the ones who still like you, or who are chasing the $5. The people who left quietly, which is who you most need to hear from, mostly won't reply. A response rate and answer mix from volunteers says little about all 5,000.
- **The credit adds bias.** A credit pulls in people who want credit and rewards fast, careless clicking. It can also push people to give the answer they think you want.
- **20 questions is long.** Expect drop-off and rushed answers by question 8 or so.
- **Asking people to explain their own past choices is weak evidence.** They tend to rationalise.

**What I'd do instead**

1. **Start from the decision.** What will you change depending on the answer: onboarding, pricing, recipe variety, delivery flexibility, win-back emails? If you can tell me the decision, I can tune the questions. Also, "stop using after the first month" needs defining: cancelled, skipped, or just inactive?

2. **Look at what you already have, since it's free.** Check these first:
   - Cancellation reasons and support tickets.
   - Usage data: when do people last order or skip, and which weeks, boxes or recipes come before the drop-off?
   - App reviews.
   - Compare retained vs churned users on things like first-box ratings, skips, delivery area and acquisition channel.

   This often points at where to look before you talk to anyone.

3. **Run 6 to 10 interviews with lapsed users, per segment.** Segments might be cancelled vs went quiet, or by household type. These are 30-minute calls about the past: "Tell me about the last time you ordered. What happened that week? What did you do for dinner instead? When did you decide to stop?" Don't pitch fixes or ask "would you come back if...". Ask to see how they actually handled meals. The credit is fine here (a gift card of more than $5, since it's more of their time), but recruit across the board, including people who didn't open your emails, via a short message, text or in-app prompt, so you don't only get the friendly ones.

4. **Then, if you need numbers, send a short survey (5 to 8 questions).** Use the themes from the interviews as the answer options, add "other" and "none", keep each question to one thing, ask what they did rather than what they might do, and pilot it on two people first. Send it to a random sample of lapsed users, with a reminder, rather than only volunteers. Now it can say roughly how common each reason is.

5. **Test fixes by behaviour, not opinion.** If interviews suggest, say, a pause option, try it as a fake-door button or small manual pilot and count who uses it before building.

**Caveats I'd put in any report**

Eight interviews can tell you what problems exist and why they happen. They can't tell you what share of churn each causes. The survey can only roughly do that, and only for those who answer. I'd rank findings by how many people hit them, how badly, and how confident we are, and keep a quote behind each.

If you'd rather keep the survey as is, the minimum fixes are: cut it to under 10 questions, add open-text "why did you stop?" and "other", sample randomly instead of emailing everyone, and note that the results describe responders only.
