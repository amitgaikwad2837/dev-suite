A survey is the wrong first tool for a "why" question, and the plan as written has several problems. Here is what I'd change.

**Why the survey won't answer your question**

1. **A multiple-choice survey can only report reasons you already thought of.** "Why did they leave" is an open question. If you write the answer options yourself, you get back your own guesses. The real reasons are often ones you wouldn't list, such as "the box arrived when I was travelling", "cooking took longer than the recipe said", or "I got tired of the same ingredients".
2. **People are poor at explaining their own behaviour in a checkbox.** They pick the tidy answer ("too expensive") when the real cause was a bad first delivery, a recipe that flopped, or simply forgetting the app existed.
3. **Twenty questions is too long.** Completion will drop sharply, and the people who finish are not typical of the people who left.
4. **Your sample will be skewed.** The $5 credit attracts bargain-hunters. It also reaches only people who still open your email. The users who left angry or indifferent are the ones least likely to reply, and they are the ones you most need to hear from.
5. **A credit is a nudge to come back.** It can bias answers toward being polite and may muddy later retention numbers.
6. **5,000 emails is a lot of reach for little insight.** You could get a thousand responses and still not know why.

**What I'd do instead: interviews first, survey second**

1. **Start with your own data (a day or two).** Look at when people drop off. Is it after the first box, the second, or when a promo ends? Compare people who stayed with people who left on first-order experience, plan type, delivery issues and support tickets. This tells you where to look.
2. **Interview 8 to 12 churned users.** Use 30-minute calls, with a bigger incentive than $5 (a $25 to $50 gift card is normal for a real conversation). Recruit across segments: left after week 1, left after week 4, left after a pause, and a couple who left after a bad delivery.
   - Ask them to walk through the last time they ordered, and then what happened after. Ask about specific events, not opinions.
   - Avoid "would you..." and "why didn't you..." questions. Use "tell me about the last box you didn't finish" and "what did you do instead that week?"
   - You'll usually hear the same four or five themes by interview 6 to 8.
3. **Then run a short survey to size what you found.** Keep it to 5 to 8 questions, with options taken from what people actually said in interviews, plus an "other (tell us)" field. Add one open-text question. Now the survey tells you how common each reason is, which is its real strength.
   - Send it to a random sample, not all 5,000. Stratify by tenure and plan type.
   - Consider a short exit prompt at cancellation as well. It catches people at the moment they decide.
4. **Compare against current users too.** If you only talk to churned users you can't tell which problems are unique to them. Ask a handful of retained users the same questions.

**If you still want to send the survey**

- Cut it to 8 questions or fewer, with at most one or two open-ended ones.
- Randomise answer order, include "none of these / other", and never make questions required.
- Don't tie the incentive to a particular answer, and say plainly that the credit is a thank-you regardless of what they say.
- Offer the credit as an entry or a flat thank-you for everyone who completes it, and track response rate by segment so you know who is missing.
- Pilot it with 20 to 30 people first to catch confusing wording.

**Rough timeline and cost**

- Data review: 1 to 2 days
- Recruiting and 10 interviews: about 1 to 2 weeks
- Synthesis (group the quotes into themes): 2 to 3 days
- Targeted survey to size the themes: about 1 week

That is cheaper than a 5,000-user mailout with credits (potentially several thousand dollars if even a quarter respond) and it will give you answers you can act on.

**Bottom line:** don't send the 20-question survey as your first step. Interview a small number of churned users to find out why, then use a short survey to measure how widespread each reason is.
